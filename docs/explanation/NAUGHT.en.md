# On the Problem of Logic

I know mathematics is hard to understand. No one admits it, but mathematics is not for everyone. Even so, I believe mathematics is the closest tool humankind can have, in power of expression, to describe the laws of this old universe. And the things you discover should not be buried under a layer of makeup so thick that no one can understand them without two pages of LaTeX wrapped around their head and tail.

But mathematics in particular, and every discipline of logic that compresses information to shorten its form of expression in general, suffers from a problem that cannot be cured: you build a Tower of Babel for your own logic, and then you pray that the world never gets hit by a nuclear bomb. Why? Because if just one generation of humankind breaks away, all the elegance, all the wonder you have made will become "Maya glyphs", waiting for some old man (and let us hope he has a cat) to decipher them.

Logic should not be compressed until it can no longer be read.

Notation exists for the convenience of the writer, but notation must never make a riddle for the reader.

This holds for mathematics, for your code, for this relic of mine, and for all knowledge that wants to survive the catastrophes of demography.

I have no quarrel with Mr. Lamport, and I do not think TLA+ is an ugly thing. But he takes accidental complexity to be the complexity of logic itself, and that is what I oppose. Mathematics is no excuse for him and his disciples alone to keep the power of expressing logic for themselves.

Logic should be something one can take in, with a cognitive ceiling just high enough that the logic stays whole while it is still laid out plainly.

A student should not be frightened by TLA+ (its syntax), nor struggle to read the notation while they rebuild, at the same time, the TLA+ state machine that their teacher wrote on a PowerPoint slide.

A student should think about the logic of the state machine first, and only then write it down on paper, in a form as ergonomic for the mind as possible (no decoration, no looking up the syntax, no flood of log traces dumped in their face). And then their friend, who knows no formal language at all, can still vaguely guess that they wrote it wrong, and scold them for being a fool.

That is why I made Naughtian Edda. As the Prose Edda kept and spread the art of the Skalds, it gives humans, artificial intelligence, and machines alike a common Rosetta Stone on which to look at a state of logic together. And when they need to, a human or an AI can turn that Rosetta Stone over and see the small, finite, but beautiful world beneath it (the original TLA+).

Think, write, share, and refine until it is whole. This beauty should reach many hands.

Golang did it (lean, explicit expression, the highest quality). Rustc did it (an interface that shields humans from LLVM IR). There is no reason Naughtian Edda cannot do it too, is there?
